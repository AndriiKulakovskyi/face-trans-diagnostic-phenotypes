\section{Validated strata: a transdiagnostic continuum}
\label{sec:strata}

The previous chapters gave us a map: every one of the $9{,}013$ patients placed on nine dimensions ---
severity, two kinds of biology (metabolic, inflammatory), cognition, sleep, mania, suicidality, developmental
adversity, substance --- with diagnosis deliberately held out. This chapter asks the question the whole
``precision psychiatry'' field chases: do patients fall into a handful of natural \emph{types} --- biotypes ---
that cut across the old diagnostic labels? Half the literature claims exactly this (``we found three biotypes
of depression,'' and so on). The discipline here is to \emph{not assume the answer}.

\subsection{Ask whether structure exists before imposing it}
The classic error in clustering clinical data is to pick a number of clusters $K$ and then ``find'' $K$
groups --- the algorithm always returns them, whether or not the data contain any. So before doing any
clustering, we run a \textbf{structure-discovery gate} (Figure~\ref{fig:gateflow}): a battery of independent
tests that each ask, in a different language, \emph{is there cluster structure at all?} Only if they converge
on ``yes'' does a biotype claim become admissible.

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\structuregateflow
\caption{Structure-gate-first. Before clustering, a battery of independent tests asks whether discrete
structure exists. On the FACE coordinates the verdict is a \emph{continuum}; the continuum is then represented
two complementary, uncertainty-propagating ways --- eight extreme phenotypes (the lead) and a coarse four-region
soft tessellation. The gate is the stratification analogue of the map chapter's ``the eligibility map is itself a result.''}
\label{fig:gateflow}
\end{figure}

Crucially, every step propagates the map's per-patient \emph{uncertainty} rather than discarding it. The gate
runs over the map's posterior draws; the soft tessellation is fit by \textbf{Extreme Deconvolution}, a
measurement-error Gaussian mixture
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:xd}
x_i \;\sim\; \sum_{k}\pi_k\,\Normal\!\left(m_k,\;V_k + S_i\right),
\end{equation}
in which each patient's \emph{known} posterior covariance $S_i$ is added inside the component --- so the
mixture clusters what patients \emph{are}, after the noise in how they were measured is accounted for. (A
full-$N$ projection first extended the three non-Gaussian axes to all $9{,}013$ patients under the fixed
certified parameters, reproducing the certified latent at Pearson $r\approx 1.00$ --- no re-fit, no imputation.)

\begin{methodsbox}[Converting a hidden assumption into a tested result]
Asking ``is there structure?'' before clustering turns the buried premise ($K$ biotypes) into a reported
finding. The gate combines tendency (Hopkins), topology (Mapper), modality (Hartigan's dip), density
(HDBSCAN), and model selection (gap statistic, mixture BIC, archetype scree). The synthesis is
conservative --- it defaults to ``continuum'' unless the evidence \emph{converges} on clusters.
\end{methodsbox}

\begin{keyresult}[There are no biotypes --- the space is a graded continuum]
Five completely different methods --- density-based clustering (HDBSCAN: $0$ clusters), the gap statistic
($K=1$), a modality test (Hartigan's dip, $p\approx 0.99$), the archetype scree (no elbow), and the mixture's
model evidence (a flat BIC basin) --- all independently say the same thing: \emph{no discrete groups}. The
nine-dimensional cloud is one smooth, graded mass; patients spread out continuously rather than clumping into
islands.
\end{keyresult}

This is the central finding of this chapter, and it has teeth precisely because it is honest and contrarian. It says most
published ``biotype'' results are probably artefacts of forcing a cluster count onto data that has none ---
which is exactly why those results famously fail to replicate --- and it lands squarely where serious
psychiatry has been heading (the dimensional RDoC/HiTOP view). The archetype scree
(Figure~\ref{fig:scree}) makes the absent $K$ visible: explained variance climbs smoothly with no knee.

corners).}
\label{fig:scree}
\end{figure}

\subsection{A continuum still has a shape: eight extreme phenotypes}
\label{sub:archetypes}
No clusters does not mean no structure. A continuum still has a \emph{shape}, and the shape has corners ---
\textbf{extreme phenotypes}.

\begin{definitionbox}[The colour-palette analogy]
There is no discrete set of ``the colours,'' yet there are pure extremes --- the primaries --- and every real
colour is a blend of them. Likewise the patient cloud has no natural groups, but it has \emph{corners}: pure
extreme phenotypes, with every patient a blend. The right description of a patient is not ``which box?'' but
``what mixture of extremes?'' --- both more honest and closer to how clinicians actually think.
\end{definitionbox}

Archetypal analysis recovers $A=8$ extreme phenotypes (cross-seed Tucker congruence $0.999$) that map almost
one-to-one onto the axis extremes (Table~\ref{tab:archetypes}, Figure~\ref{fig:profiles}): a maximally
inflammatory type, a maximally manic type, a maximally metabolic type, a maximal childhood-adversity type, a
sleep type, a high-severity cognitive-impairment type, a rare extreme-suicidality type --- plus a low-burden
pole where everything is mild.

The key fact about a continuum is the blending. Most patients do not \emph{sit} at a corner
(Figure~\ref{fig:simplex}).

\begin{figure}[h]\centering
\archetypesimplex
\caption{The continuum-with-corners. The cloud (ellipse) has extreme-phenotype corners --- the
\emph{what kind} (biology in teal: metabolic, inflammatory) --- and is stretched along a severity spine, the
\emph{how much}. Dots are patients: $75\%$ live in the interior as blends of corners. Two patients at the same
point on the spine (same severity) can sit near opposite corners (biologically opposite).}
\label{fig:simplex}
\end{figure}

\begin{keyresult}[Most patients are blends, not corners]
$75\%$ of patients have no single dominant archetype (max simplex weight $<0.5$; mean normalized entropy
$0.67$): they live in the \emph{interior} between corners. Hard assignment would be a fiction --- the honest
decision object is the \emph{distribution} over phenotypes, exactly what the continuum and the map's per-patient
uncertainty demand. (The coarse four-region tessellation --- low-burden 31\%, high-severity$+$metabolic 32\%,
low-metabolic/better-cognition 25\%, mania$+$developmental$+$sleep 12\% --- assigns more confidently, $92\%$,
precisely because it tiles broad regions rather than resolving corners.)
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{Severity is the spine, not a corner}
\label{sub:spine}
The most elegant detail: \textbf{overall severity has no corner of its own.} Geometrically it is the
\emph{spine} --- the axis the entire cloud is stretched along (Figure~\ref{fig:simplex}) --- and every
archetype sits at some severity level. This is the bifactor structure of the map made visible as geometry: severity
is the \emph{how much}, the specific axes (biology, cognition, sleep, mania, \dots) are the \emph{what kind},
and they are orthogonal. So a ``maximally inflammatory'' patient and a ``low-burden'' patient can be at the
\emph{same} severity --- they differ in what is wrong, not how much. The validation confirms it quantitatively:
the partition's variance-explained is led by the \emph{specific} axes (mania $\eta^2$ $0.45$, developmental
$0.35$, metabolic $0.21$, sleep $0.19$, cognition $0.17$) more than by severity ($0.31$) --- the maximum
specific axis exceeds $G$.

\subsection{Biology is its own thing --- it survives into the phenotypes}
\begin{keyresult}[Distinct metabolic and inflammatory extreme phenotypes]
This is the whole reason the project exists, and it survives all the way into the phenotypes: metabolic and
inflammatory load form their \emph{own} distinct extreme corners --- separate from each other and from the
severity spine. Two patients who look equally ill can be biologically \emph{opposite} phenotypes. A map that
only knew ``how sick are they?'' would be blind to this --- and that heterogeneity is exactly what a
biology-aware stratification is supposed to capture.
\end{keyresult}

\begin{caveatbox}[Honest footnote: the inflammatory extreme is rare]
Only $\approx 1.9\%$ of patients sit near the inflammatory corner (and $1.5\%$ near suicidality) --- the long
tails of skewed latents. They are real and \emph{distinct}, which is why the resolution was kept fine enough
($A=8$) to see them, but their per-patient weights carry wide uncertainty. Substance, by contrast, never forms
a corner: as the noisiest, two-cohort axis (no DR data) it self-down-weights, appearing only as a side-loading
on the inflammatory phenotype --- a non-result that was predicted, and is consistent with the model.
\end{caveatbox}

\subsection{The DSM-5 showdown}
We then put the map head-to-head against the diagnostic labels --- all \emph{seven} DSM-5 subtypes, not just
the three cohorts (Table~\ref{tab:m2valid}, Figure~\ref{fig:m2valid}). Two clean results.

\textbf{The structure shares almost zero information with DSM-5.} The adjusted Rand index --- a measure of how
much two groupings agree, scaled so that $0$ is chance agreement and $1$ is identical --- between the strata
and the seven subtypes is $\approx 0$ ($0.020$ tessellation, $0.05$ archetypes); every phenotype is a full mix
of diagnoses (Cram\'er's $V$ $0.18$--$0.28$ --- weak gradients only). The high-severity$+$cognitive corner
draws the most schizophrenia patients \emph{and} the most major-depression patients --- two textbook-different
diagnoses converging on one phenotype --- while the mania corner is almost all bipolar, as you would expect,
but as a gradient, not a wall. The diagnoses are shuffled right through the cloud.

\textbf{The map describes patients far better than the labels.} The seven diagnostic boxes explain only
$\approx 5\%$ of where a patient actually sits in this biological/cognitive space; the data's own regions
explain $\approx 21\%$, with \emph{fewer} groups --- and a free four-region mixture beats a DSM-5-constrained
seven-group mixture on model evidence (BIC $199{,}325$ vs $206{,}016$). The diagnoses barely organize this
landscape at all.

\begin{caveatbox}[The line we did not cross: ``describes better'' is not ``clinically better'']
We have \emph{not} shown the map predicts outcomes or guides treatment better than diagnosis. Those are the
questions of the prognosis and treatment chapters, and overclaiming them now would be the field's besetting
sin. What this chapter earns is a \emph{descriptive} win and the right setup for the real test --- and it
reframes the question sharply: not ``are our clusters the true kinds?'' but ``does a continuous, biology-aware
representation out-predict the seven DSM-5 subtypes on course and treatment response?''
\end{caveatbox}

\subsection{Why trust it: the structure is about patients, not measurement}
\begin{keyresult}[Membership is not a missingness artefact]
The classic trap in clustering messy clinical data is accidentally grouping patients by \emph{who got which
tests} rather than what they are like --- people with bloodwork versus without form fake ``biotypes.'' We
tested exactly that: a classifier predicting a patient's stratum from the \emph{pattern of what was measured}
achieves $0.248$ accuracy against a $0.323$ majority baseline --- a \emph{negative} lift, worse than chance.
Membership is governed by what patients \emph{are}, not by what was measured on them. The whole
measurement-error apparatus --- propagating each patient's uncertainty the entire way (Eq.~\ref{eq:xd}) --- did
its job. This vindicates the map's ``propagate the uncertainty'' design.
\end{keyresult}

\subsection{The one picture to hold in your head}
The UMAP embedding says it all (Figure~\ref{fig:umap}): one diffuse cloud, with bipolar, schizophrenia, and
depression patients thoroughly intermixed (no diagnostic territories), and two smooth gradients crossing it in
different directions --- a severity gradient and a biological gradient. Not a constellation of islands; a
\emph{landscape with a ridge and crossing slopes}.

\subsection{What the stratification establishes --- and what it defers}
This chapter converts the map's coordinate system into decision-region objects --- soft phenotype memberships
with uncertainty --- and establishes, under \emph{internal/descriptive} validity, that they are real, stable,
transdiagnostic, specific-axis-driven, not a missingness artefact, and a tighter description of the data than
the diagnostic labels. The honest boundaries are explicit:

\begin{openbox}[Internal/descriptive validity only --- actionability comes later]
The stratification does \emph{not} claim the strata are natural kinds (they are a continuum), nor any
predictive, prognostic, temporal, or treatment value. The granularities ($A=8$ archetypes, $K=4$ regions) are
parsimony choices, not discovered counts; two corners (inflammatory $1.9\%$, suicidality $1.5\%$) are real but
rare; and the embeddings are visualization-only. The decisive tests come in the later chapters:
\textbf{temporal coherence} (do the coordinates and memberships \emph{persist} across follow-up visits, and
more stably than a DSM-5 diagnosis?), \textbf{prognosis} (do the strata add \emph{incremental predictive value
beyond diagnosis $+$ severity}?), and \textbf{treatment} (do the strata \emph{moderate treatment response}?).
This chapter is the description; the later chapters test whether the description is clinically useful.
\end{openbox}
